Keeping Your Menomonee Falls Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on descaling every year if their water is hard.

How old is your water heater? The rating plate on the tank usually includes the month and year it was built, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank models reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units about 20. If your heater is past that point and also shows rusty water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than a rushed emergency install once the tank fails.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both inexpensive fixes. The same drip ignored for months can keep stressing the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Common Water Heater Problems in Menomonee Falls

Water heaters in and around Menomonee Falls deal with a predictable set of stresses. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Then there's age. When the protective anode rod wears out, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam often shows up next. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Code requirements add another layer. Swapping out an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: an expansion tank if the plumbing is a closed system, a relief valve drain line piped to an approved spot, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Well water brings its own challenges, such as heavier sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that sometimes develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most cost far less when caught early.
