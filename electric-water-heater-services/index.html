An electric water heater has no burner, no flame, and no vent, so it is tempting to think nothing much can go wrong. In practice, the parts that do the work (two heating elements, two thermostats, and a high-limit safety switch) fail in predictable ways. The upper thermostat heats the top third of the tank first, then switches power to the lower element. If the upper element or thermostat fails, the lower one never gets its turn, and you get no hot water at all. If the lower element burns out, the top of the tank still heats, so you get a short burst of hot water that runs out fast. For Waukesha, WI homeowners, knowing which symptom you have is the first step toward the right fix.

Electric water heaters are easy to test when you know what to measure, and that is exactly how Waukesha Water Heater starts. At each Waukesha home, our technicians measure element resistance, check for elements shorted to the tank, test thermostat operation, confirm the breaker and supply voltage, and inspect every connection from the junction box to the terminals. We also look at sediment. A lower element buried in mineral buildup runs hot, fails early, and can make the tank noisy, so whenever we pull an element, we check how much sediment is sitting on the bottom and flush it out if needed.

Once we know what failed, the fix is usually quick. Elements, thermostats, and high-limit switches are standard parts, and our trucks carry the common sizes. Replacing an element means shutting off power, draining the tank, installing the new element, and refilling completely before power is restored, which protects the new element from a dry fire. We also repair damaged wiring, tighten or replace terminal connections, and flush sediment from the tank bottom. If replacement makes more sense, we install new electric heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, confirm the breaker and wire gauge are correct, and add the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code requires. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Cold water everywhere, even after waiting
  • Popping, sizzling, or crackling sounds from the tank
  • Browned wiring or a burnt odor from behind the access cover
  • A breaker that trips when the heater calls for heat
  • A red reset button that keeps tripping
  • A shower that turns lukewarm much sooner than it used to
  • Rust-colored hot water or moisture around the heater
  • Water that comes out scalding hot

Prevention Tips That Actually Help

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Drain a few gallons from the bottom of the tank periodically to control sediment
  • Test the temperature and pressure relief valve once a year
  • Never turn the breaker back on until the tank is completely full of water
  • Keep the thermostats set around 120 degrees to save energy and reduce scalding risk
  • Ask for an anode rod check every few years, since the anode is what keeps the tank from rusting
  • Get a diagnosis for any reset button that trips more than once
  • Protect your elements from hard water with a softener or upgraded low-watt-density elements
