Most gas water heaters in Waukesha, WI work the same basic way. Gas enters through a control valve that doubles as the thermostat and feeds a burner beneath the tank, and the hot exhaust travels up a flue through the middle of the tank and out through the vent. A thermocouple, or a flame sensor on newer electronic models, tells the valve a flame is present; without that signal, the valve shuts off the gas for safety. The design is dependable, but it has a few predictable weak spots. Thermocouples wear out, pilot tubes and burner ports clog, the flame arrestor screen at the base packs up with lint and dust, control valves fail, and vents lose their draft.

Every gas water heater call starts with safety and then moves to testing. If you ever smell gas, leave the home first, call the gas utility from outside, and do not operate switches or try to relight anything. Once the area is safe, our Waukesha technicians work through the heater systematically: millivolt readings on the thermocouple, a close look at the pilot and main burner flames, gas pressure at the control valve, a draft test to make sure exhaust is not spilling back, and a CO check around the appliance. Scorch marks at the burner access, rust flakes on the burner, and a lazy yellow flame each point to a different problem, and we chase down the right one.

Once we know the cause, the repair is usually simple. A new thermocouple or flame sensor, a cleaned pilot tube and burner, a cleared flame arrestor screen, or a replacement gas control valve gets most heaters running again the same day. Vent problems, like a disconnected connector or a flue pipe without enough rise, are corrected on the spot. If sediment is rumbling in the tank, we flush it, and if the anode rod is spent, we can replace it. When replacement is the smarter move, we install new gas heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, with proper atmospheric, power vent, or direct vent venting, a drip leg on the gas supply, and the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code calls for. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • A silent heater with no flame and no hot water
  • Popping, rumbling, or crackling sounds as the burner heats
  • Hot water that runs out faster than it used to
  • A faint gas or rotten-egg smell near the heater (leave and call the gas utility)
  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank
  • A pilot that lights, then goes out when you release the knob
  • A flame that looks orange or floppy rather than steady and blue
  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Two things make gas water heater work a poor DIY project: the parts involve fuel and combustion, and the symptoms overlap. A pilot outage might come from a worn thermocouple, a partly blocked pilot tube, lint on the flame arrestor screen, a downdraft, or an internal valve failure. A wrong guess there just wastes money. Guess wrong on a gas fitting or vent connection and the stakes are much higher, since a small leak or a spillage problem can put gas or carbon monoxide into the living space. Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Here's where to begin:

  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
  • Give the heater breathing room and keep flammables well away from it
  • Vacuum lint and dust from the flame arrestor screen at the base a few times a year
  • Choose a setting of about 120 degrees to balance comfort, safety, and efficiency
  • Flush the tank on a regular schedule so sediment never bakes onto the bottom
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years and replaced when it is worn
  • Keep working CO alarms near bedrooms and on each floor
