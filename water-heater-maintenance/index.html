Most water heaters do not simply wear out; they rust out or scale up, and both problems can be held off for years. As water heats, dissolved minerals drop out of solution and settle into a layer of sediment on the tank floor. On a gas unit, that layer sits between the flame and the water, so the steel runs hotter and the tank starts to rumble and pop. On an electric unit, it buries the lower element until the element burns out. Meanwhile the anode rod, a metal rod designed to corrode so the tank does not, is slowly used up. Once it is gone, the tank itself becomes the target. In Waukesha, WI, we see this pattern constantly, and most of it was preventable.

The work is hands-on and specific. We drain the tank and flush out sediment until the water runs clean. We pull the anode rod to see how much protection remains and replace it when needed. We lift the T&P valve lever to confirm it opens, then watch it reseat without dripping. For gas equipment, we inspect the burner and flame, pilot or ignition parts, thermocouple or flame sensor, and the vent and draft. For electric equipment, we meter the elements and check the thermostats and wiring. Then we check the expansion tank's pressure, confirm a safe temperature setting near 120 degrees, and look over every fitting for early signs of a leak.

Tankless owners need maintenance too, just a different kind. Scale builds up inside the heat exchanger, especially in hard water, and eventually triggers error codes, reduced flow, or temperature swings. We flush the heat exchanger with a pump and descaling solution through the service valves, clean the inlet filter, check the venting and condensate line on condensing models, and review the unit's error history. Warning Signs to Watch For

Most tanks announce trouble weeks or months ahead with rumbling noises, rusty water, or hot showers that keep getting shorter. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Hot water with a sulfur odor
  • Energy bills creeping up with no change in hot water use
  • Scale warnings or intermittent shutdowns on a tankless system
  • Water in the drain pan or on the floor near the heater
  • Rusty or discolored hot water at the tap
  • Less hot water available than when the tank was new
  • Rumbling, popping, or crackling from the tank as it heats
  • A water heater that has never been flushed or inspected

Why Professional Water Heater Maintenance Matters

There is a short list of things you can do yourself: watching for leaks, keeping the area around the heater clear, and noting new noises. Beyond that, the work gets riskier. Draining a tank means handling scalding water and a drain valve that may not close again if it is old and full of sediment. Pulling an anode rod often takes serious torque on a fitting that has not moved in years. Testing a T&P valve on an older heater can leave it dripping afterward. On gas units, the burner, thermocouple, and venting involve combustion safety, and a vent that is not drafting can put carbon monoxide into the house.

We come ready for the common surprises: a drain valve that will not reseal, an anode rod that has turned to wire, a relief valve that drips after testing. We carry replacements, along with meters for element and thermostat testing and a descaling kit for tankless units. More than parts, we bring a trained eye. Small signs, like rust at a connection, a weak flame, or a waterlogged expansion tank, tell us where a heater is headed, and catching them early is the entire point of the visit. That is how maintenance turns into extra years of service.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Plan yearly descaling for a tankless heater, sooner if scale warnings appear
  • Glance at the pan and connections regularly so small leaks are caught early
  • Schedule a flush every year, or every six months if you have hard water
  • Treat hard water to cut down on sediment and scale
  • Have the anode rod checked every few years and replaced when it is mostly gone
  • Avoid turning the tank up past 120 degrees unless a mixing valve is installed
  • Keep at least a couple of feet clear around the heater for air and service access
